# Problem Statement

## Background

Global and domestic supply chains depend on a complex network of ports, warehouses, routes, and fleet assets. India's logistics sector handles over ₹900 lakh crore in goods annually, flowing through 12+ major ports, thousands of km of road/rail networks, and hundreds of warehouses.

## The Problem

Supply chain operators — logistics directors, operations managers, and crisis coordinators — currently have **reactive**, not **predictive** visibility. Their existing tools show:
- What shipments are in transit
- Where they currently are
- What delays have already happened

**What they cannot do** is ask: *"What would happen if Mumbai Port closes tomorrow for 72 hours?"*

When a disruption hits, operators must:
1. Manually identify affected shipments (hours of work)
2. Guess which routes are blocked
3. Estimate delays without data
4. Choose a recovery strategy based on intuition
5. Discover cold-chain cargo is at temperature risk only after spoilage

This reactive approach costs Indian logistics companies an estimated 13–15% of total cargo value in disruption-related losses annually.

## Who is Affected

- **Logistics operations managers** at large shippers (automotive, pharma, FMCG, retail)
- **Port operations coordinators** managing congestion and alternative routing
- **Cold-chain logistics directors** responsible for pharmaceutical and food shipments
- **Supply chain risk officers** preparing business continuity plans

## Why It Matters

A 72-hour port closure at Mumbai (India's busiest port, handling ~2.4M TEUs/year) can:
- Affect 50–100+ active shipments
- Expose ₹30–50 Cr in cargo value to risk
- Create 200–400+ hours of cumulative delay
- Put dozens of cold-chain shipments (vaccines, pharmaceuticals) at temperature risk

## Why Existing Solutions Fall Short

Current supply chain visibility tools (TMS, WMS, track-and-trace) are **monitoring** systems. They show the present state but provide no simulation capability. Risk mitigation is manual, slow, and inconsistent — different managers make different decisions for the same disruption, with no data-driven comparison of recovery strategies.
